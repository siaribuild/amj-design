Read this first

Every screen the referral program adds, in the site's own design language.

This is what the program will look like. The colours, type sizes, spacing, buttons, cards and status chips are the real ones taken from the live site — the only visual difference from production is the typeface, because a mock is not allowed to fetch anything from the internet and the site's two fonts live on Google Fonts.

Every word here is real copy. If you approve this, this is what a tradie reads. Numbers shown are the launch defaults — 2.5%, 1%, $2,000, 12 months, 14 days — and every one of them is a slot filled from the ops console, so changing any of them later needs no code and no new words.

Screens are shown at real widths: desktop 1180px and phone 375px. Scroll a frame to see the whole screen where it is taller than the box.

Never a total discount

The referred tradie sees 2.5%, never 7.5%. Every registered account already gets a standing 5% that is deliberately invisible, and a total gives it away by subtraction. No screen here prints a combined figure.

Referring is never tied to buying

Nothing says "order first, then you can earn". That coupling is a strict-liability offence (ACL s 49). "Any account can refer" and "we need your bank details" are stated as two separate facts, everywhere, on purpose.

No invite form, anywhere

There is no screen on which you type a mate's name, phone or email. You share your own code, your own way. That boundary is what keeps the whole business outside a Privacy Act trigger.

The conditions sit next to the offer

Minimum order, what the 1% is on, paid-on-payment, the 12-month lapse, the ABN and the 14 days appear beside the headline — not in a footer. Fine print cannot rescue a headline claim.

Why it's built this way

The rule box is the thing they repeat. One sentence, two numbers, on a job site, from memory. Everything else on the page supports it.

The two facts are numbered and separated by a rule. "Any account can refer" and "we need your bank details" must never merge into anything that reads like "you have to be a customer to refer" — that shape is what the law is about. Numbering them makes them two facts typographically, not one sentence with a "but" in it.

The conditions grid sits directly under the hero, before the sales pitch continues. It is denser than any other section on the site, and that is deliberate: the conditions have to be next to the claim, and a tradie who reads only this grid still has the whole deal.

Every bold number is a slot. Raising the discount to 5% changes six figures on this page and not one word.

The delicate screen — how the ask is made proportionate

The headline is about money, not paperwork. "Where do we send the money?" presumes the money is coming. "Complete your profile" or "Verify your details" would read as onboarding admin, and this is the screen we can least afford to have read that way.

What they get is stated before what we want. The right-hand panel shows the deal and a greyed-out example code — a preview of the thing on the other side of the form, not a teaser.

Four fields, one screen, one button. No steps, no progress bar, no second page. On a phone it is a single scroll.

The three quiet lines under the button are load-bearing. The first is the legal separation — it says the ask has nothing to do with buying. The second and third answer the two questions a tradie actually has about handing over bank details: who sees this, and can I undo it.

Worth saying out loud

This screen is real friction placed in front of the share action, and some tradies will not bother. That is the trade you took to make sure the business never holds money it cannot pay out. The design does everything it can to make the ask feel proportionate, but no amount of copy makes "give us your bank details before you've earned a cent" free. If reach turns out to matter more than the unclaimed-money exposure, the decision to revisit is D18, not this screen.

Reading order, and what the list refuses to say

Money first. Three cells in the dashboard's own summary-strip component: Pending, Confirmed, Paid. Confirmed is the only one that carries a tone, because it is the only one with a date attached to it.

The confirmed cell always names the date the payment is due — the promise we made in the offer, restated where it can be checked.

Never a dollar figure against a person. The list carries a business name (or a masked email when they haven't set one) and a status word, and stops. What their order was worth is theirs.

The code drops to the bottom once it's working. On an empty account it's the point of the screen; on a working one, the earnings are.

A constraint that fights the screen — please read

The legal research points out that a commission percentage is invertible: if a referrer can see "$45.00 earned" and "1%" on the same screen, they can work out that their mate spent $4,500. Its cheapest suggested fix is not to print the rate beside the earned figure; but the spec requires the rate to be stated wherever the offer is made, including here.

The design takes the research's stronger option instead: tell the referred tradie up front. A line on their discount panel and on the code-applied confirmation says their mate is notified when their first order is paid and is paid a percentage of it. Nobody is surprised, so nothing needs hiding. As cheap insurance the rate and the earned figures are also kept in different blocks and never in the same sentence — but the disclosure is the real answer, and it needs your sign-off, because it means every referred tradie is told the referrer earns a percentage of what they spend.

The rules this panel is built on

One percentage, and no other. 2.5%, never a combined figure, never a reference to any other discount. This is the single thing most likely to get "improved" into a total later — a total gives away the standing discount by subtraction just as effectively as printing it.

No redemption language anywhere. Not apply, redeem, claim, use at checkout, voucher, credit or balance. There is no redemption moment in this system — the discount is applied when the price is computed — and copy that implies otherwise creates support calls and an expectation of a further reduction on top of the price already shown.

The expiry is in the headline, not the footnote. Under 30 days it becomes the headline itself, the spine turns amber, and the CTA changes to finishing the quote. That is also when the reminder email goes out.

Nothing on this panel moves with the ex/inc GST toggle, because a percentage has no GST dimension. That is arithmetic, not an exemption — the prices it acted on keep honouring the preference everywhere else, and if a worked dollar figure is ever added here it moves with the preference like any other price.

One thing this panel can't fix

A referred tradie has nothing to check "2.5% off" against. This site publishes no list price, so the figure is unverifiable by design — which is exactly what makes it safe to show the increment alone, and also what means its credibility rests entirely on tone. It is worth knowing that a tradie who priced a job anonymously, then signed up with a code, will watch the price drop by more than 2.5% (the standing account discount arrives at the same moment). That gap exists today for every registering customer; the referral is what gives them a number to compare it against.

Two things the queue does that a staff member shouldn't have to remember

Anything past its promised date sorts to the top and is flagged. The 14 days is a promise made in the offer; a weekly run must not quietly let one slip past it.

Accruing money can't age out of sight. If a payout threshold is ever switched on, any group whose oldest confirmed earning passes eleven months is promoted into the run regardless — a month of margin before it would become a statutory problem.

Full bank details are visible here and in the CSV, and every unmasked read is logged. There is no screen for that log, by design.

Before you approve

Four things worth a decision, and one you can't have

1 · The payment-details gate is the program's biggest cost

Asking for an ABN and bank details before a tradie has earned anything will lose some of them. Surface 1 State B and Surface 3 State A are designed to make the ask feel proportionate — money-first headline, what-you-get before what-we-want, four fields, one button, and three lines that answer "who sees this, and can I undo it". That is everything design can do. If reach turns out to matter more than never holding unpayable money, the thing to revisit is the rule, not the screen.

2 · Telling the referred tradie that their mate earns a percentage

A commission percentage is invertible: rate plus earned amount equals their mate's order value. The design's answer is to say so up front — on the code-applied confirmation and on the discount panel: "Kirra Glazing is told when your first order is paid, and is paid a percentage of it." That sentence needs your approval, because it means every referred tradie knows the referrer profits from their spend. The alternative — hiding the rate from the earnings screen, or showing banded amounts — costs the referrer clarity about their own money, which is the whole point of that screen.

3 · The GST sentence on the payout is a placeholder position, not a settled one

Everywhere the payout is described, the copy says "Amounts include any GST payable." That follows the decision already taken, but a bare stated figure defaults to GST-inclusive in law and the exact wording is an accountant question. The words are in a slot that can be swapped without touching layout — but they must not ship unreviewed, because "we pay you 1%" with nothing said about GST is the one formulation that is definitely wrong.

4 · Two things that could not be satisfied and were not worked around

A referred tradie cannot verify their discount. The site publishes no list price, so "2.5% off" has nothing visible to be off. That is precisely what makes showing the increment alone safe — but it also means the claim rests on trust rather than on arithmetic, and a tradie who priced a job anonymously before signing up will see the price move by more than 2.5%.

"Any account can refer" and "we need your bank details" is a confusing pair on a phone. The law requires them to be separate facts, and this design separates them typographically everywhere. It cannot make them feel like one simple rule, because they aren't one.

What changes if you change a number

Nothing in this mock is written around 2.5% or 1%. Raise the discount to 5%, drop the minimum to $1,000, shorten the window to six months — every figure on every screen re-renders and not one sentence needs rewriting. That is why the numbers are always followed by a noun ("2.5% off their first order", "$2,000 ex GST", "12 months") rather than fused into the grammar.
